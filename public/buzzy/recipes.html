Recipes

A recipe is your way of doing something, written down well enough that BeeZilla can do it your way next time. Below are twenty examples of the kind of recipe you can build here — each one is a real, ordinary job people already do by hand. The steps shown are the basic shape, not the finished thing. Yours gets sharper every time you correct it.

How a recipe gets written here

You don't sit down to author a recipe. You do the job, and your corrections become the recipe.

  1. Start a job and describe the goal in your own words.
  2. BeeZilla interviews you — the problem, who it's for, what's in the way, what done looks like.
  3. It shows you a full draft, not a plan. You change what's wrong.
  4. Every change is a rule: what it should have known, what it should never do.
  5. When the result is right, BeeZilla offers to save it as a recipe — with its checks.
  6. Next time, it shows the checks first: "14 of 14 keywords covered," "line items total $1,284 — reconciles."
  7. After a few clean runs, the recipe can work on its own up to its boundary — never past it.

The five questions a recipe answers: When should this be used? · What must I always get from you? · What do you check that a beginner wouldn't? · What must never appear in the result? · What should BeeZilla refuse to do without you?

Career & earning

01Example

A résumé aimed at one posting

One page tuned to a single job ad, plus a short cover note and a list of what you changed.

  1. Paste the posting and your current résumé.
  2. Pull out its must-have keywords and the seniority it implies.
  3. Map each must-have to something you actually did; drop what doesn't map.
  4. Rewrite the top third so the match is obvious in seven seconds.
  5. Put a number in every bullet you can — people, money, time, volume.
  6. Cut the material that belongs to a different job.
  7. Write three sentences naming the role and your closest match.

You'd know it worked if: every must-have appears in context, no claim lacks your number, and it's still one page.

02Example

An interview pack from the job description

The questions this employer will actually ask, and one true story behind each answer.

  1. Paste the posting and the résumé you sent them.
  2. List the eight questions this role invites.
  3. Attach one real story to each: situation, what you did, what changed.
  4. Write the honest answer to the gap they'll spot.
  5. Prepare three questions that show you understand their problem.
  6. Rehearse once out loud; trim anything past ninety seconds.

You'd know it worked if: every question traces to the posting, every answer uses a story you actually lived, and nothing is invented.

03Example

A raise or rate conversation

The number, the case for it, and what to say when the answer isn't yes.

  1. State your role, location, current pay, and what you're asking for.
  2. Collect two independent ranges for that role and market.
  3. Write one line on what you delivered and what it changed.
  4. Decide your walk-away number and one non-salary ask.
  5. Script the opening ask, then the silence after it.
  6. Prepare three branches: yes, not now, no — one follow-up line each.

You'd know it worked if: two sourced numbers (or an honest "couldn't confirm"), all three branches written, and no threats anywhere in it.

04Example

A career pivot, in plain language

Your experience translated into the language of the field you're moving into.

  1. List your roles and what each one actually built.
  2. Name the target role and its top five requirements.
  3. Build a translation table: old title language → new field language.
  4. Pick three proof stories that survive the translation.
  5. Write the sixty-second answer to "why this move?"
  6. Retire the jargon only your old industry understands.

You'd know it worked if: every claim traces to a real role and the table covers all five requirements.

Money & admin

05Example

A subscription and bill audit

Every recurring charge, sorted into cancel, downgrade, renegotiate, keep.

  1. Paste the last two statements, or list the charges.
  2. Flag anything duplicated, raised, or unrecognized.
  3. Sort each line: cancel, downgrade, renegotiate, keep.
  4. Write the cancellation or renewal line for each one.
  5. Do the cheapest wins first.
  6. Log what you saved and the date it lands.

You'd know it worked if: the line items add up to the total you claim, and no saving appears without a source line.

06Example

A dispute, in the right order

The letters to send, in sequence, with the deadline on each one.

  1. Describe what went wrong, with dates and amounts.
  2. Attach what you have: bill, contract, receipt, photo.
  3. Set the ladder: the company, then the regulator, then the card issuer.
  4. Write letter one — facts, what you want, by when.
  5. Draft each follow-up with its own date.
  6. Stop and refer to a professional if it turns into a legal claim.

You'd know it worked if: the dates are consistent, the amounts match the evidence, and no rule or statute number was made up.

07Example

An insurance claim packet

A chronology, a photo set, and the policy language that matches the loss.

  1. Give the policy number, the date, and what happened.
  2. Write the chronology one event per line, in order.
  3. Match each damaged or lost item to a policy section.
  4. List the photos an adjuster will ask for, then take them.
  5. Ask the one coverage question you need answered.
  6. Hand coverage disputes to a professional.

You'd know it worked if: every line cites a policy section or a photo, and no value was invented.

08Example

A monthly close for a small business

The month's books, categorized the same way every time, with the questions you ask the owner.

  1. Export the month's transactions.
  2. Write a rule for every merchant you've seen before.
  3. Apply the rules, then look at what's left over.
  4. Ask about the leftovers only — five questions, maximum.
  5. Reconcile the bank balance.
  6. Save a one-page summary: income, spend, the three things that moved.

You'd know it worked if: nothing is uncategorized, the account reconciles, and every flagged item has a named question.

Marketing & sales

09Example

A local search pass

The business profile fixes, photos, and weekly habit that make a service business findable nearby.

  1. Name the service, the city, and the three things you want to be found for.
  2. Check the category, hours, service area, and phone number.
  3. Rewrite the description: what you do, for whom, where.
  4. Add ten real photos — storefront, work in progress, finished, the team.
  5. Set up the review ask and reply to every review.
  6. Post one update a week, about work you actually did.

You'd know it worked if: every recommendation maps to a field that exists, and nothing promises a ranking.

10Example

An offer, positioned for one niche

Who it's for, what it replaces, what it costs, and who it isn't for.

  1. Pick one audience and the problem they'd pay to end.
  2. Name what they use now and why it fails them.
  3. Write the one-line offer: outcome, for whom, how fast.
  4. Anchor the price against two real comparisons.
  5. List the three proof points you can actually show.
  6. Write the "we're not for you" line.

You'd know it worked if: there's exactly one audience, every proof point is sourced, and no claim is unfalsifiable.

11Example

An outreach sequence you'd answer

Three touches, four objections answered, and a break-up line. Drafted, never sent for you.

  1. Define the exact recipient and the trigger you're responding to.
  2. Write a first line that is specific and true — not a compliment.
  3. Write three touches: value, proof, break-up; four to five days apart.
  4. Answer the four objections you actually hear.
  5. Add the one-line opt-out.
  6. Draft only. You press send.

You'd know it worked if: every claim is verifiable, no scraped personal detail appears, and the send boundary held.

12Example

A product listing that gets found

Title, bullets, images, and backend terms rebuilt for one marketplace category.

  1. Paste the current listing and the three listings above you.
  2. Find the terms your category actually searches with.
  3. Write the title in the category's formula: brand, product, key attribute, size.
  4. Rewrite bullets: benefit first, specification second.
  5. Reorder images: hero, in use, scale, detail, comparison.
  6. Fill the backend fields with the terms that didn't fit.

You'd know it worked if: every field is inside its limit, no prohibited claim appears, and every term came from real search data.

Home, health & family

13Example

Contractor scope and bid comparison

A scope so complete that the bids actually compare, and the questions that expose a bad one.

  1. Describe the job and what "done" means to you.
  2. Write the scope lines that prevent change orders: materials, disposal, permits, cleanup.
  3. Get at least two written bids against the same scope.
  4. Normalize them line by line.
  5. Ask: who pulls the permit, what's excluded, what changes the price.
  6. Choose on the total, not the headline.

You'd know it worked if: two sourced bids (or an honest "couldn't confirm"), the comparison adds up, and exclusions are listed.

14Example

A care coordination packet

One page a new doctor can read in a minute, plus the questions for each appointment.

  1. Collect conditions, medications, and allergies exactly as written.
  2. Build the one-page history in the order a clinician reads it.
  3. Write the question for each upcoming appointment.
  4. Record what changed after each visit.
  5. Keep one folder and one naming convention.

You'd know it worked if: no dosage or diagnosis was invented, every medication has a source, and the professional-referral line is intact.

15Example

A week of meals from constraints

Five dinners that share ingredients, fit the budget and the clock, and survive the allergies.

  1. Set the constraints: people, budget, time ceiling, allergies.
  2. Pick five dinners that share ingredients.
  3. Build one shopping list from those five.
  4. Note the prep you can do once, on Sunday.
  5. Plan the leftovers onto a specific night.

You'd know it worked if: the list adds up, every ingredient is used or deliberately parked, and allergens were checked on every line.

16Example

A move, six weeks out

The dates that matter, the order they matter in, and what it will cost.

  1. List the fixed dates: lease, movers, utilities, mail.
  2. Work back six weeks, one task per week.
  3. Separate "before the truck" from "after the truck".
  4. Price it in lines: movers, supplies, deposits, travel.
  5. Write the first-night box list.

You'd know it worked if: no task depends on a later date, the cost lines total, and every deposit has a refund condition.

Craft & life events

17Example

The family recipe, made repeatable

The dish you cook by feel, written so someone else gets the same result.

  1. Write it as you actually make it, vague cues included.
  2. Turn each cue into something testable: temperature, time, colour, sound.
  3. Write the ratios so it scales up and down.
  4. Add the substitution table.
  5. Name the three ways it goes wrong and how to spot each.

You'd know it worked if: the ratios scale, the steps are in order, and no instruction says "until it looks right."

18Example

An event on a hard budget

Headcount, cap, and the three things people will actually remember.

  1. Set the date, the headcount, and the cap you won't cross.
  2. List what guests remember: the food, the room, one moment.
  3. Split every line: vendor, or do it yourself.
  4. Get quotes for the vendor lines.
  5. Write the rain plan and the run sheet with times.

You'd know it worked if: the budget sums to the cap, every vendor is quoted or flagged, and the run sheet has clock times.

19Example

The "if something happens" folder

What your family would need to find, in what order — and where it lives, never the passwords.

  1. List the accounts, policies, and documents someone would need.
  2. Write where each one lives — not the password, the location.
  3. Add the people to call, in order.
  4. Record the decisions already made.
  5. Set one review date a year.
  6. Send wills and estates to an attorney.

You'd know it worked if: no secret is stored in it, the coverage list is complete, and the referral line survived your edits.

20Example

The job only you know how to do

The task you've never written down, turned into steps someone else can finish.

  1. Pick the task you're the only one who can do.
  2. Film yourself doing it once, start to finish.
  3. Break it into steps, each with a "done" you can see.
  4. Add the quality bar: what good looks like at each step.
  5. Add the three mistakes to prevent.
  6. Have someone else run it and mark where they got stuck.

You'd know it worked if: someone else completes it unaided, every step has a visible done-state, and the stuck points are fixed.

These are examples, not templates you're meant to follow. Two people doing the same job will write different recipes, and the difference is the whole point — it's your judgment that gets saved, not ours. Recipes that touch legal, medical, tax, or insurance decisions always carry a "stop and see a professional" line, and that line can't be edited out.
